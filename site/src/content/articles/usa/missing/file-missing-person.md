Contact the police where the person was last seen, by 911 if they may be in danger, and file a missing person report. There is no legal waiting period for anyone under 21, and California's police training agency calls the "wait 24 hours" idea a common myth.

Missing person reports in the United States are taken by local, county, state, and tribal police agencies, and each state adds its own rules on top of federal law. Here is what federal law sets, what differs by state, and how to get your report in.

## Is there a waiting period?

**For anyone under 21: no, by federal law.**

- Every federal, state, and local law enforcement agency must report each case of a missing child under the age of 21 to the FBI's National Crime Information Center (NCIC), and also to the NamUs databases (34 U.S.C. § 41307).
- States must ensure that no law enforcement agency keeps a policy requiring a waiting period before accepting a missing child report (34 U.S.C. § 41308(1)).
- The report must be entered into the state system, NCIC, and NamUs within 2 hours of receipt (34 U.S.C. § 41308(3)).
- Within 30 days, the agency must verify and update the record, adding medical and dental records and a photo taken in the past 180 days where available (34 U.S.C. § 41308(4)(A)).

**For adults 21 and older: it depends on the state and agency.** The U.S. Department of Justice guide *When a Loved One Goes Missing* (November 2021) says there is no standard, national protocol for how local police respond to missing adults over 21. Some states go further than federal law. California's Penal Code § 14211(a), as summarized in California POST's 2024 guidelines, makes it the duty of all law enforcement agencies to immediately assist anyone trying to report a missing person or runaway, and requires the report to be accepted regardless of jurisdiction. The same guidelines call the idea that 24 hours (or any other time frame) must pass before police will accept a report a "common myth."

Your state's law may be different. The Justice Department guide says to contact police as soon as possible to file a report, whatever the person's age.

## Who to call

The Justice Department guide says:

- **Call 911** if there is evidence or suspicion of foul play, if the person is in danger, or if there is an immediate risk of injury or death. Examples it gives: an older person with cognitive disabilities, or someone missing in extreme heat or cold.
- **Call the police non-emergency number** where the person went missing if there is no immediate danger, or if the person has been missing for a long time.

Florida's Department of Law Enforcement gives the same basic rule: contact the local law enforcement agency where the missing person was last seen. If the person went missing on tribal land, the Justice Department guide says to contact the tribal police department if there is one, or else the local police or county sheriff.

## How to file

You can report in person, by phone, or online, according to the Justice Department guide. Give as much as you can:

- a recent photo
- name, nicknames, date of birth
- height, weight, hair and eye color, scars, marks, tattoos
- clothing and items they had
- last known location, time, and companions
- likely destinations
- medical conditions, disabilities, medications
- vehicle details
- phone numbers and social media accounts

For a child, the federal statute lists what the police record must include: name, date of birth, sex, race, height, weight, eye and hair color, a recent photo if available, the date and location of last contact, and the category under which the child is reported missing.

## After you file

- **Get the details.** Write down the officer's name and badge number, the date and time, and the case report number. Ask for a copy of the report.
- **Keep the investigator updated.** Share any new information, and tell police right away if the person comes home or contacts you.
- **If police won't help.** The Justice Department guide says to file a complaint with the supervisor or supervising entity: for city police, the police chief; for county police, the sheriff.
- **Know adults' rights.** The guide notes that adults have the right to leave. When police find a missing adult alive, the person must consent before police share their location with the family.

## Use NamUs

The National Missing and Unidentified Persons System (NamUs), administered by the Justice Department's National Institute of Justice (NIJ), is a central database and support program for missing, unclaimed, and unidentified person cases. According to NIJ, families can create an account to enter their missing loved one; NamUs staff then verify the report with law enforcement, or connect the family with an agency if a report still needs to be made. The Justice Department guide adds that if police have not gathered biometric information such as DNA, families can contact NamUs regional specialists for support.

The Justice Department guide lists NamUs at 833-872-5176 and namus@usdoj.gov.

## For missing children

After you report to police, the AMBER Alert program's FAQ says to call the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children at 1-800-THE-LOST (1-800-843-5678).

Whether an AMBER Alert or another public alert is issued is up to law enforcement and depends on your state's criteria. See our article on AMBER Alerts and Silver Alerts.
