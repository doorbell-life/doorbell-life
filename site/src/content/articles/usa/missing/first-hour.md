Call the police right away and file a missing person report. You do not have to wait 24 hours, and for anyone under 21 federal law forbids police from making you wait.

The rest of the first hour is about giving police what they need and keeping evidence intact. The steps below come from U.S. Department of Justice guides for families. Details of how police respond are set by each state and each agency, so ask the officer what happens next in your area.

## 1. Call 911 or the police now

The Justice Department guide *When a Loved One Goes Missing* (November 2021) says to dial 911 when:

- there is evidence or suspicion of foul play, or your loved one is in danger; or
- there is an immediate risk of injury or death, including a missing older person with cognitive disabilities, or someone missing in extreme heat or cold.

If there is no immediate danger, call the police non-emergency number for the place where the person went missing. The same guide says to dial 911 or contact police immediately whenever the missing person is younger than 21.

### There is no waiting period for children and young people

Under federal law, every federal, state, and local law enforcement agency must report each case of a missing child under the age of 21 to the FBI's National Crime Information Center (NCIC) (34 U.S.C. § 41307). States must make sure no agency keeps a policy that requires a waiting period before accepting a missing child report, and the report must be entered into NCIC within 2 hours of receipt (34 U.S.C. § 41308).

For adults over 21, the Justice Department guide says there is no standard national protocol, so responses differ between agencies. Report anyway. Some states set their own rules; check your state's missing persons clearinghouse or state police.

## 2. Have this information ready

The Justice Department guide lists what to gather for police. You do not need everything before you call; share what you have.

- A recent photo
- Full name, nicknames, date of birth, and age
- Height, weight, hair and eye color, scars, marks, and tattoos
- What the person was wearing and carrying
- When and where they were last seen, and who they were with
- Places they might go: favorite spots, work, school, friends' homes
- Medical conditions, disabilities, and medications
- Phone numbers, social media accounts, and email addresses
- Vehicle make, model, and license plate
- Items left behind, such as a wallet, phone, or keys
- Any earlier times they went missing and how those ended

## 3. Do not clean up

The OJJDP *Family Survival Guide* checklist tells parents to limit access to the home until police arrive and not to touch or remove anything from the child's room. Clothing, sheets, computers, and even trash may hold clues. The Justice Department guide for adults gives similar advice: secure rooms such as a bedroom or bathroom, because police may need to collect items.

## 4. Write things down

Keep one notebook for everything. Record:

- the police department's name and phone number
- the officer's name and badge number
- the date and time you filed the report
- the case report number

Ask for a copy of the report. The OJJDP checklist also suggests asking for the name and phone number of the investigator assigned to the case.

## 5. If the missing person is a child

The OJJDP checklist says to ask police to enter the child into NCIC and to put out a "Be On the Look Out" (BOLO) bulletin. The checklist dates from 1998; the Office of Justice Programs published a fifth edition with a 2025 update that, according to its summary, keeps the first-48-hours checklist and stresses that there is no waiting period to report a missing child.

After you have reported to police, the AMBER Alert program's FAQ says to call the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children at 1-800-THE-LOST (1-800-843-5678).

An AMBER Alert is not automatic. Police decide whether a case meets their state's criteria. See our article on AMBER Alerts and Silver Alerts.

## 6. While you wait

- Have one person stay by the home phone. The OJJDP checklist suggests one person answers calls and logs each one.
- Call friends, relatives, and anyone the person might contact, and pass new leads to the investigator.
- If the person comes home or contacts you, tell police right away so they can confirm the person is safe, as the Justice Department guide advises.
- Look after yourself and the rest of the family. Eat, rest when you can, and ask others to help.

## Before you post online

Sharing a flyer can help, but check with police first. The Justice Department guide says to coordinate with law enforcement before using social media, and to post the police tip line, not your personal phone number or address. See our article on sharing missing person alerts without causing harm.
