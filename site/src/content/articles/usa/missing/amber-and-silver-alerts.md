An AMBER Alert is an urgent public bulletin about an abducted child; a Silver Alert is a state alert for a missing older adult, usually one with dementia. Both are issued by law enforcement under state plans, and the rules differ from state to state.

Neither alert is something a family can send out on its own. If someone is missing, report to police first; police decide whether the case qualifies.

## AMBER Alert

### What it is

According to the U.S. Department of Justice's AMBER Alert program, AMBER stands for "America's Missing: Broadcast Emergency Response." It began in 1996, when broadcasters in the Dallas-Fort Worth area teamed up with local police to build an early warning system for abducted children.

Once law enforcement determines that a child has been abducted and the case meets AMBER criteria, police notify broadcasters and state transportation officials. Alerts interrupt regular programming on radio and TV and appear on highway signs. They can also be passed on through lottery terminals, digital billboards, internet services, and mobile phones.

### Who issues it

AMBER Alerts are issued by law enforcement under state plans. The Justice Department says AMBER plans exist in all 50 states, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and the U.S. Virgin Islands, with expansion into Indian Country. A National AMBER Alert Coordinator at the Justice Department, a role created by the PROTECT Act of 2003, supports those plans but does not issue alerts.

If an abductor may have crossed state lines, the issuing state's AMBER coordinator can ask the other state to extend the alert. The program calls this a multi-state activation.

### The criteria

Each state plan sets its own criteria. The Justice Department's recommended guidance, which states may adopt voluntarily, is:

1. Law enforcement reasonably believes an abduction has occurred.
2. Law enforcement believes the child is in imminent danger of serious bodily injury or death.
3. There is enough descriptive information about the child and the abduction to issue an alert.
4. The child is 17 years old or younger.
5. The child's name and other key data, including the Child Abduction flag, have been entered into the FBI's National Crime Information Center (NCIC).

The Justice Department says most states' guidelines follow these closely, but not all. Its guidance notes that some plans have used different age limits, such as 10, 12, 14, 15, or 16.

### What it is not

An AMBER Alert is not issued for every missing child. The FAQ says alerts are reserved for the most serious cases that meet the criteria, because overuse could make the public desensitized. Police have other tools for other cases.

### How it reaches your phone

AMBER Alerts can arrive as Wireless Emergency Alerts. The Justice Department says this program is operated by the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA). Phones that support it receive alerts automatically when they are in the area where an alert was issued.

### Results

As of December 31, 2025, the Justice Department reports that 1,312 children had been successfully recovered because of an AMBER Alert activation, including at least 252 rescued because of a Wireless Emergency Alert.

## Silver Alert

### What it is

A Silver Alert is a public alert for a missing older adult, most often someone with Alzheimer's disease or another dementia. Silver Alerts are run by states, not by the federal government. A Congressional Research Service report (R47010) states that federal bills for a national senior alert system have been introduced, such as the National Silver Alert Act of 2014, but that no federal law establishing such a system has been enacted, and that several states run their own Silver or Senior Alert systems. We have not verified whether this has changed since that report.

### Who issues it

State or local law enforcement. Florida's Department of Law Enforcement, for example, says the local agency takes the missing person report and decides if a Silver Alert is appropriate, and only law enforcement can request a state Silver Alert. The Texas Department of Public Safety says only a law enforcement agency or authorized entity can request activation of its state alert network.

### The criteria differ by state

There is no single national definition. The Justice Department's National Ashanti Alert Network keeps a list of state programs, which shows how much they vary. A few examples from that list:

| State | Program listed |
| --- | --- |
| Texas | Silver Alert (65+) |
| California | Silver Alert (65+), among other advisories |
| Maryland | Silver Alert (60+), other alerts case by case |
| North Carolina | Silver Alert (50+) |
| Florida | Silver Alert (18+) and Purple Alert |
| Georgia | Mattie's Call |
| Ohio | Endangered Missing Adult Alert (65+) |

Florida's FAQ adds its own details: road signs are used mainly for people 60 and older with an irreversible deterioration of intellectual faculties, such as Alzheimer's or dementia; how police confirm that condition is up to the agency; and the state's dynamic highway message signs stay on for a maximum of 6 hours. Florida also says the Emergency Alert System is restricted to child abductions and is not used for Silver Alerts there.

To learn the rules where you live, check your state police or state missing persons clearinghouse.

## Ashanti Alert

There is also a federal network for adults who fall outside both alerts. A Justice Department document describes the Ashanti Alert Act of 2018 (Public Law 115-401) as establishing a voluntary nationwide communication network for missing persons over age 17 who fall outside the scope of AMBER Alerts and Silver Alerts. As with the others, alerts go through law enforcement.

## What you can do when you see an alert

- Note the description of the person, any vehicle, and the plate number.
- If you see them, call 911 or the number in the alert. Florida's FAQ asks people to note the location, direction of travel, and plate.
- Do not approach a suspect yourself.
- Share the official alert, not a screenshot, so updates and cancellations reach people too.
