Stay with them in a safe, public spot and call 911 or local police. Speak calmly and wait with them until officers arrive.

Someone may already be looking for this person. Police can check missing person reports and alerts you cannot see, so the call is the most useful thing you can do.

## Step 1: Decide if it is an emergency

The U.S. Department of Justice guide *When a Loved One Goes Missing* (November 2021) says to dial 911 when someone is in danger or at immediate risk of injury or death, and names two situations that fit:

- an older person with cognitive disabilities who is missing
- anyone out in extreme heat or cold

A young child alone, an older adult who cannot say where they live, or anyone near traffic or water: treat it as an emergency and call 911. If the situation is calm and nobody is in danger, you can call the local police non-emergency number instead.

## Step 2: Stay with them, in view of others

- Stay where you are or move a short distance to a safe, well-lit public spot, such as a store entrance or a front desk.
- If you are in a store, mall, park, or venue, tell staff or security. North Dakota's child safety guidance teaches children who get separated in a public place to go to a checkout counter, security office, or lost and found, so staff there are a natural first stop.

## Step 3: Talk to an older adult gently

Many older adults who seem lost have Alzheimer's disease or another dementia. The National Institute on Aging (NIA) gives these do's and don'ts for talking with a person who has Alzheimer's:

**Do:**

- Make eye contact and use the person's name if you know it.
- Listen and be open to their concerns.
- Give them extra time to respond.
- Try different words if they do not understand the first time.
- Ask yes-or-no questions, such as "Would you like some water?" instead of "What do you want?"

**Don't:**

- Speak in an angry or tense tone.
- Interrupt or argue with them.
- Talk about them as if they are not there.
- Ask whether they remember something or someone.

Look, without searching their pockets, for a medical bracelet or a label in their clothing. NIA advises caregivers to give people who wander an ID bracelet with a phone number, or to label their clothes, so this information is often there. Give it to the dispatcher.

## Step 4: Talk to a child simply

- Get down to their eye level and tell them you are going to call for help.
- Ask for their name and a parent's name. North Dakota's guidance tells parents to teach children their full names, addresses, and phone numbers, so older children may know them.
- Do not ask them to come with you somewhere else. North Dakota teaches children not to go anywhere with anyone unless their parents said it was OK, so a child who refuses is doing the right thing.

## Step 5: Tell police what you saw

When you call, share:

- exactly where you are
- what the person looks like and is wearing
- anything they told you, including names and places
- if they seem hurt, sick, cold, or confused
- the time you first noticed them

Stay on the line or stay put until officers arrive, unless staying puts you in danger.

## If they match an alert you saw

Florida's Department of Law Enforcement asks the public, during a Silver Alert, to note the person's description and to call 911 right away if they see the person or vehicle, noting the location, direction of travel, and plate number. It also says that if you come across an older adult who seems lost or disoriented, contacting law enforcement may help prevent a Silver Alert in the first place.

For a child named in an AMBER Alert, call 911 or the number in the alert. AMBER Alerts are issued by law enforcement for abducted children who meet the alert criteria, so do not approach a suspect or a vehicle yourself.

## Other numbers

- **Emergency:** 911
- **National Center for Missing & Exploited Children:** 1-800-THE-LOST (1-800-843-5678), listed by USAGov and the AMBER Alert program for missing children
