Make it harder to leave unnoticed, and make it easy to be found. Lock and alarm the doors, never leave someone with a history of wandering alone, and make sure they always carry ID with your phone number.

The National Institute on Aging (NIA) says many people with Alzheimer's disease wander away from their home or caregiver. No single step prevents it, so NIA recommends two layers: changes that slow someone down at the door, and safeguards that help them get home if they do get out.

## Layer 1: Make leaving harder

NIA's tips to prevent wandering:

- **Lock the doors.** Consider a keyed deadbolt, or add a second lock placed high or low on the door. If the person learns to open it, change the latch or lock. Keep a key nearby for emergencies.
- **Use signs.** Put STOP, DO NOT ENTER, or CLOSED signs on doors.
- **Know when a door opens.** Install a smart doorbell or an alarm that chimes when a door is opened.
- **Secure the yard.** Use fencing and a locked gate.
- **Limit windows.** Install safety devices that limit how far windows open.
- **Hide the cues.** Keep shoes, keys, suitcases, coats, and hats out of sight. These can signal "time to go."
- **Do not leave them alone.** NIA says not to leave a person with Alzheimer's who has a history of wandering unattended.

## Layer 2: Make finding them easier

NIA's tips to keep a wandering person safe:

- **ID at all times.** The person should carry some kind of ID or wear a medical bracelet with their name, address, and your phone number.
- **Label clothing.** If they might take off a bracelet, put a name and phone number on their clothes.
- **Consider GPS.** NIA notes many GPS tracking systems are available, as an alternative or an addition to a medical bracelet.
- **Tell the neighbors and the police.** Let neighbors and local police know the person tends to wander.
- **Keep a recent photo or video.** It helps police if the person gets lost.
- **Look into a safe return program.** NIA mentions the MedicAlert + Alzheimer's Association Safe and Found service and lists 800-432-5378 to find a program in your area. NIA notes this program is not affiliated with NIA and may charge a fee.

## Prepare a "go sheet" now

If your loved one does go missing, police will ask for details fast. The U.S. Department of Justice guide *When a Loved One Goes Missing* (November 2021) lists what to give police. Write it down today and keep it with a recent photo:

- Full name, nicknames, and date of birth
- Height, weight, hair and eye color, scars, marks, and tattoos
- Medical conditions, medications, and any disability
- Places they like to go, past homes, old workplaces
- Vehicle make, model, and plate, if they still drive
- Their phone number and any tracking device details
- What happened in any earlier wandering episodes

NIA also recommends keeping a recent photograph or video of the person on hand for police.

## If they go missing: call 911 right away

The Justice Department guide says to dial 911 when there is an immediate risk of injury or death, and it names a missing older person with cognitive disabilities as an example, along with anyone missing in extreme heat or cold. Do not wait to search on your own first.

Tell the dispatcher about the dementia diagnosis. Florida's law enforcement agency, for example, says families should tell police if they believe the missing person has dementia, because that can affect whether a Silver Alert is issued there.

## Silver Alerts depend on your state

Many states run an alert for missing older adults with dementia, often called a Silver Alert, but the name, age limit, and rules differ. The Justice Department's National Ashanti Alert Network lists each state's program: for example, Silver Alert (65+) in Kansas and Texas, Silver Alert (60+) in Maryland and Nevada, Silver Alert (50+) in New Mexico and North Carolina, and different program names in states such as Georgia, Michigan, and Ohio. Only law enforcement can request these alerts. Check your state police or attorney general's website to learn the rules where you live.
