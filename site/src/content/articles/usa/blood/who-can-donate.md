Most healthy people who weigh at least 110 pounds and meet their state's minimum age, usually 16 with a parent's consent or 17, can donate whole blood. The final decision is made at the blood center on the day you give.

Eligibility in the U.S. is set in layers. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) sets federal minimums that every blood center must follow. State law sets the minimum age. Each blood center can add its own rules. The American Red Cross says most donor eligibility rules come from the FDA, while others are set by medical staff at individual blood centers, so rules may differ between programs.

## The federal baseline (FDA)

Under 21 CFR 630.10, a blood center must determine on the day of donation, before collecting, that you are in good health. At minimum:

| Check | Federal standard |
| --- | --- |
| Weight | At least 50 kg (110 lb) |
| Temperature | No higher than 37.5 °C (99.5 °F) |
| Blood pressure | Systolic 90–180 mm Hg, diastolic 50–100 mm Hg, unless a physician examines you and approves |
| Pulse | Regular, 50–100 beats per minute, unless a physician approves |
| Hemoglobin | At least 12.5 g/dL for female donors, 13.0 g/dL for male donors (or equivalent hematocrit). Some centers may accept female donors from 12.0 g/dL under an FDA-accepted procedure |
| Arm | Needle site free of infection, inflammation, and lesions |

You also must:

- **not be pregnant**, and not have been pregnant within the past 6 weeks
- **show proof of identity** and give a postal address where you can be reached for 8 weeks
- **wait long enough since your last donation**: at least 8 weeks for whole blood, 16 weeks after a double red cell donation (21 CFR 630.15)

The interviewer must also turn you away that day if you appear to be under the influence of drugs or alcohol, or if you say you are donating to get test results.

## Age: varies by state

The federal rules above do not list a minimum age, and the age you can start donating depends on the state. The American Red Cross's guidance for young donors says you must be at least 17 in most states, or 16 with parental consent in some states. Its state list shows that Delaware, Hawaii, and New Mexico do not allow 16-year-olds to donate. That same page gives two different counts of states that require parental consent for 17-year-olds (six in one place, eight in another), and the Red Cross's main eligibility page says "at least 16 years old in most states" for whole blood. Because the wording differs, check your state's rule with your blood center.

The Red Cross adds extra height and weight requirements for donors 18 and younger and for all high school students.

## Health questions

Before you donate, you answer a private health history interview. Federal rules require it to cover, among other things:

- symptoms of a recent or current illness
- certain medical treatments or medications
- travel to or residence in areas where certain transfusion-transmitted infections are common
- risk factors for infections such as HIV

On illness, the Red Cross says to reschedule if you don't feel well and that you can come back 24 hours after symptoms pass. On medications, it says most will not disqualify you, but some require a waiting period after your last dose. On travel, it says you may be deferred if you lived in or traveled to a malaria-risk country in the past three years.

## The HIV risk questions changed in 2023

In May 2023, the FDA issued guidance recommending that blood centers ask every donor the same individual risk-based questions. Under that guidance, a donor who reports a new sexual partner, or more than one sexual partner, in the past 3 months **and** anal sex in the past 3 months is deferred for 3 months. The guidance also recommends asking all donors about medications to treat or prevent HIV.

The Red Cross says it has eliminated questions based on gender and sexual orientation, and has also eased past deferrals for people who spent time in Europe.

## Other donation types have different rules

The Red Cross lists different requirements for each donation type:

| Type | How often (Red Cross) | Extra requirements (Red Cross) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Whole blood | Every 56 days, up to 6 times a year | 110 lb or more |
| Power Red (double red cells) | Every 112 days, up to 3 times a year | Male donors: at least 5'1" and 130 lb. Female donors: age 19–74, at least 5'3" and 150 lb |
| Platelets | Every 7 days, up to 24 times a year | At least 17 in most states, 110 lb or more |
| AB Elite plasma | Every 28 days, up to 13 times a year | Type AB blood, at least 17, 110 lb or more |

The Red Cross notes the number of allowed donations per year may be lower because of red cell and plasma loss limits.

## If you were turned away before

The Red Cross says that even if you were deferred in the past, you may be eligible now. For example, it says people turned away for low iron may be able to donate in the future, and that many medications only require a waiting period. Ask the center when you can come back.

## The bottom line

Requirements in this article are minimums and examples. Your blood center makes the final call on the day you donate. If you have a specific question, such as about a medication or a medical condition, call the center before your appointment.
