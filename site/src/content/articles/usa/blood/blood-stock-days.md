It is how many days a blood center's current stock would last at normal hospital demand. America's Blood Centers treats 3 or more days as enough for normal operations, 2 days as low, and 1 day or less as critically low.

You will see this measure in news reports and blood drive appeals: "a one-day supply of type O," "less than two days on the shelf." It is a quick way to compare inventory with how fast hospitals use blood.

## How to read the numbers

America's Blood Centers publishes a daily inventory report. Its national widget, checked on November 3, 2026, says there are 59 community blood centers in America and explains the levels this way:

| Days of supply | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| 3 or more days | Enough blood to meet normal operating demands |
| 2 days | Running low; donations needed |
| 1 day or less | Critically low; donations needed as soon as possible |

The color key on the same page uses green for centers reporting a 3+ day supply, yellow for a 1–2 day supply, and red for a 0–1 day supply.

America's Blood Centers adds two caveats: all blood centers need donations even when supply is adequate, and the national data reflects trends, so your local blood center's needs may differ.

## Why even a few days is tight

Blood cannot be stockpiled for long or made quickly:

- **It takes time to be ready.** America's Blood Centers says it takes 24 to 36 hours for a donation to be ready for a patient, so blood must already be on hand for emergencies.
- **It expires.** The American Red Cross says red blood cells must be used within 42 days and platelets within 5 days. America's Blood Centers gives platelets a shelf life of 5 to 7 days.
- **It cannot be manufactured.** The Red Cross notes that blood and platelets can only come from volunteer donors.
- **Demand is steady.** The Red Cross estimates that about 29,000 units of red blood cells are needed every day in the U.S., along with nearly 5,000 units of platelets and 6,500 units of plasma.

Put together: blood donated today cannot help a patient today, so what is already on the shelf has to cover the gap.

## Days of supply differ by blood type

A center can be fine overall but short on one type. In a July 27, 2026 press release, the American Red Cross said it had less than a one-day national supply of type O positive blood, the most commonly transfused type, and had begun limiting type O distributions to hospitals. It declared a national blood supply crisis, only the second in its history; the first was in January 2022.

The Red Cross explains why type O matters: it accounts for about 60% of Red Cross blood distributions. Type O positive can be safely given to about 80% of patients, and type O negative is the universal type used when there is no time to check a patient's type. Only about 7% of people in the U.S. are type O negative, according to the Red Cross.

## When shortages tend to happen

America's Blood Centers says shortages can happen at any time but are especially common during the winter holidays and summer. In July 2026, AABB, America's Blood Centers, and the American Red Cross issued a joint statement that the U.S. blood supply was lower than it should be for routine and emergency care, and that many blood collection organizations had faced or were facing a critical shortage that summer. The Red Cross release that month pointed to extreme heat, poor air quality, and foodborne illness as factors lowering donor turnout.

## Numbers come from more than one organization

Blood in the U.S. is collected by several organizations. The American Red Cross says it provides about 40% of the nation's blood and monitors its own national supply daily; America's Blood Centers reports on community blood centers. A "days of supply" figure from one organization describes that organization's inventory, so check who is reporting before comparing numbers.

## What it means for you

- If your local center reports 2 days or less, it is asking for donors now.
- If your type is the one running short, especially type O, your donation is likely needed most.
- Even at 3+ days, regular donors keep the buffer from shrinking. The Red Cross says only about 3% of age-eligible people donate blood each year.
