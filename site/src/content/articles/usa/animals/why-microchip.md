A microchip is permanent ID that can't fall off like a collar, and shelters and vets scan for it, so it gives a lost pet a much better chance of getting home; it only works if you register the chip and keep your contact information current, and dogs entering the U.S. must have one.

## What a microchip is, and what it isn't

A pet microchip is about the size of a grain of rice. In the U.S. it is usually implanted under the skin between the shoulder blades. According to the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA, a professional association of veterinarians):

- The chip has no battery. It stays inactive until a scanner passes over it, and then it sends back a unique ID number.
- The chip stores only that number, not your name or address. Your contact information lives in a registry database linked to the number.

That means a microchip is **not** a GPS tracker. It can't show where your pet is. It helps only after someone finds your pet and has it scanned.

## Why it helps

**Shelters scan for it.** LA Animal Services (City of Los Angeles) says every animal admitted to its shelters is scanned for a microchip. If one is found, staff contact the microchip company and then reach out to the owner.

**It can't come off.** The AVMA describes microchips as permanent identification that can't be lost or removed like a collar or tag. LA Animal Services recommends a collar and ID tag too, but notes that tags often fall off.

**Return rates.** LA Animal Services' microchipping page (checked November 3, 2026) states that microchipped dogs are more likely to be returned to their owners and that microchipped cats are 20 times as likely to be returned. The same page says LA Animal Services reunited 3,347 pets with their families in 2025. The page does not cite the study behind the "20 times" figure.

## The catch: register it and keep it current

The AVMA says a microchip is "only as effective as the information linked to it." If your registry information is missing or outdated, your pet may not get home even after it is scanned. LA Animal Services makes the same point: when a microchipped pet isn't returned, it's usually because the owner information in the registry is wrong or missing. Its page also says only 6 out of 10 microchips are registered.

What to do:

1. **Find your chip number.** It's usually in your adoption or vet records. If not, a vet or shelter can scan your pet.
2. **Find the registry.** The AVMA points to the American Animal Hospital Association's Microchip Registry Lookup Tool, which tells you which registry a chip number is associated with.
3. **Update your details.** Contact the registry when you move or change phone numbers. LA Animal Services suggests listing your mobile number, since people tend to keep it when they move.
4. **Check it regularly.** The AVMA recommends asking your vet to scan the chip at routine checkups to make sure it's still there and working. It also promotes August 15, "Check the Chip Day," as a yearly reminder to review your registry information.

## Getting a microchip

Your veterinarian can implant one. The AVMA notes that vets are trained to implant chips safely and correctly. Some public shelters offer microchipping too. For example, LA Animal Services offers microchipping to City of Los Angeles residents at any of its six centers, with no appointment, for $15 (price as listed on its page, checked November 3, 2026). Prices and services vary by location.

Pets adopted from public shelters often already have a chip. LA Animal Services microchips every cat, dog, and rabbit it adopts out, and includes the cost in the adoption fee. Montgomery County, Maryland, includes a microchip in its adoption fee for dogs and cats. You still need to make sure the chip is registered in **your** name after adoption.

## Which kind of chip

The AVMA says there is no single U.S. standard for pet microchips; chips differ in radio frequency and ID-number length. The AVMA and many other organizations support chips that meet International Organization for Standardization (ISO) standards. These use a 15-digit ID number and operate at 134.2 kHz. That matters most if your pet might ever travel abroad.

## Travel: required for dogs entering the U.S.

Microchips are required for some travel:

- **Coming into the U.S.:** according to the CDC, all dogs entering or returning to the United States must be at least 6 months old, be microchipped, appear healthy on arrival, and have a CDC Dog Import Form receipt. Dogs that have been in a high-risk country for dog rabies in the past 6 months must meet additional requirements. This applies to U.S. dogs coming home from a trip, too.
- **Leaving the U.S.:** the CDC Yellow Book notes that, depending on the destination country, pets may need ISO-compliant microchips, updated vaccinations, parasite treatments, or blood tests. USDA APHIS lists the export requirements for each country.

## Is a microchip required by law?

Outside of travel, any requirement comes from your state, county, or city, and we have not verified a complete list (not yet verified). What many cities and counties do require is a **license**, which is separate from a microchip.
